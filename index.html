The big grazers are the gist

what keeps the grass

A prairie is kept by grazing and fire. Bison eat the grass and leave most of the flowers, roll out wallows that hold rain, and move on so the grass can rest; that is how the tallgrass stayed tallgrass for about ten thousand years. A lawn is the same ground held still by mowing, fertiliser and gasoline. In the someday half, the animals do the work the mowers did: sheep crop the old lawns and verges on the block, young shepherds move them every morning, and a bison herd grazes the commons and the park. The barbecue is where the neighbourhood shares what the land and the herd gave.

  • Not all grazers are alike. On the same prairie, bison and cattle leave different plant communities: cattle linger near water and shade and eat more of the flowers.
  • Goats browse instead of grazing. They eat leaves, vines and brush, so cities hire goat herds to clear invasive thickets and steep slopes where mowers can't go. Texas raises more goats than any other state. In the festival scene they work the edge of the Thicket.
  • Sheep go for the flowering plants more than the grass, which is why towns and ranchers hire flocks for verges, weeds and old lawns, and keep the bison on the prairie.

City Barbecue Festival

the Beautiful Hensel Thicket, near campus

The whole city gathers in the Beautiful Hensel Thicket for Tejano barbecue, the best in the land and as close to a sacrament as food gets.

  • Barbacoa. In South Texas, barbacoa is traditionally a beef head wrapped in leaves or burlap, laid on mesquite coals in a pit in the ground, covered with earth and left to cook overnight. It comes up on Sunday morning and goes into tortillas with onion, cilantro and salsa, usually with family. Few places still cook it in a real pit; the festival keeps one going.
  • Fajitas. South Texas ranch hands were often given the cheap cuts, skirt steak among them, and grilled them over mesquite. Faja means a belt or strip.
  • Pan de campo. Camp bread from the cattle drives, baked in a skillet or Dutch oven; the official state bread of Texas since 2005.
  • Aguas frescas and raspas. Horchata, jamaica and watermelon in big glass jars, and shaved ice with syrup for the kids.
  • Conjunto. Button accordion and bajo sexto. The accordion reached South Texas with German and Czech settlers in the 1800s, and Tejano musicians made it the sound of the border.
  • The running gag. Slide left and the festival is today: a leaf-blower crew is clearing the paths, a riding mower is doing laps, and you can barely hear the band. Slide right and the crew is gone, the herd is keeping the grass, and the music comes through clear. Two-Stroke Morning has the decibels.

How the later block makes a living

1

Very good at one thing? That's a living.

The block needs bread, plates, honey, cloth, wheels and music, and pays the people who make them well. If you're very good at one thing, you can make a living doing it.

2

Nobody needs it right now? You're supported.

When the need for your work dips, the block keeps you going until you find a purposeful use of your time again: learning a neighbour's craft, teaching, building. Nobody has to pretend to be busy.

3

Some work is everyone's.

Daily jobs nobody would do all day, like moving the flock, are shared out and paid. Young people start on them at about 13, the way a paper route used to be a first job: every day, rain or shine, trusted with something.

Where would the support come from? This page doesn't settle that. People have tried versions of it for a long time: medieval craft guilds kept relief boxes for members who were sick or out of work; friendly societies and unions did the same; most countries now run unemployment insurance; and basic-income trials, such as Finland's in 2017 and 2018, found that people's wellbeing improved while their employment barely changed.

How the 1957 block made a living

for comparison

Mostly one paycheck per house, often from a big employer; a mortgage made cheap by federal loan programmes; and a car, a TV and a refrigerator bought on monthly payments. If the plant laid you off, unemployment insurance paid part of your wage for a set number of weeks. Those cheap loans and new houses were, by federal underwriting rules and by clauses in the deeds, mostly closed to Black families, which is why this block's 1957 side looks the way it does.

What's real in the 1957 half

the details aren't made up
  • Duck and cover. A 1951 civil-defence film made for the US Federal Civil Defense Administration and shown in schools, alongside classroom drills.
  • The triangles on the radio dial. Radios sold in the US from 1953 to 1963 marked 640 and 1240 kHz with small triangles: the CONELRAD frequencies to tune to in an air raid.
  • Television. About 9% of US homes had a TV in 1950 and about 87% by 1960 (Nielsen figures, as compiled by the Television Bureau of Advertising).
  • Fallout shelters. Civil-defence plans for home shelters circulated through the 1950s; the big national push came in 1961.
  • The kit. The kettle charcoal grill appeared in 1952, the frozen TV dinner in 1953, the pocket transistor radio in 1954, coonskin caps swept the playgrounds in 1955, and the plastic pink flamingo landed on lawns in 1957.
  • Who got the houses. The Federal Housing Administration's underwriting manual steered loans away from mixed neighbourhoods, and the first big postwar suburbs wrote whites-only clauses into their leases and deeds. The Supreme Court made those clauses unenforceable in 1948, but builders and lenders kept the pattern for years.
  • Town flocks. Several European cities, Paris among them, now hire sheep to graze parks and verges instead of mowing them.
